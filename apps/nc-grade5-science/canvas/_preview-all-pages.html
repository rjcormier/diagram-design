PS.5.1 · Matter and Its Interactions · PS.5.1.1

When you tear, cut, mix, dissolve, melt or rearrange something in a closed system, the total weight stays the same.

NC objective PS.5.1.1: Carry out investigations to compare the weight of objects before and after an interaction.

I can measure and compare weight before and after something changes, and use the numbers to show that matter is conserved.

Big idea

When you tear, cut, mix, dissolve, melt or rearrange something in a closed system, the total weight stays the same. Matter does not disappear. Sugar stirred into tea seems to vanish, but the scale proves it is still there.

Key ideas
  • The weight of an object equals the weight of all its parts added together.
  • In a closed system, weight before an interaction = weight after.
  • If the weight drops, something left the system (like water vapor or a gas escaping an open cup).
  • Use the right tool (a balance or scale), measure carefully, and do more than one trial.

PS.5.1 · Matter and Its Interactions · PS.5.1.2

Some changes only change shape or state, like dissolving sugar or crushing a can.

NC objective PS.5.1.2: Carry out investigations to explain whether the mixing of two or more substances results in new substances.

I can look for evidence (color change, odor, bubbles, temperature change) to decide whether mixing made a new substance.

Big idea

Some changes only change shape or state, like dissolving sugar or crushing a can. Other changes make a brand-new substance with different properties, like rust on a nail or bubbles from baking soda and vinegar. Either way, the total weight does not change.

Key ideas
  • Physical change: same substance, new shape or state (melting, dissolving, crushing).
  • New substance: properties change. Look for color change, new odor, gas bubbles, or temperature change.
  • Record observations in a before-and-after chart.
  • No matter what change happens, the total weight of the substances stays the same.

PS.5.1 · Matter and Its Interactions · PS.5.1.3

Heating and cooling change materials, but not all materials respond the same way.

NC objective PS.5.1.3: Carry out investigations to compare how heating and cooling affect some materials and how this relates to their purpose and practical applications.

I can use temperature data to compare conductors and insulators, and explain why we choose certain materials for certain jobs.

Big idea

Heating and cooling change materials, but not all materials respond the same way. Metals are conductors and move heat quickly. Foam, wood and cloth are insulators and slow heat down. That's why pots are metal and pot handles are wood or silicone.

Key ideas
  • Heating and cooling can cause changes in state, expansion and contraction.
  • Conductors (like copper and other metals) let heat move through them quickly.
  • Insulators (like foam, wood, silicone) slow heat transfer.
  • Many changes happen faster at higher temperatures.
  • Builders leave gaps in sidewalks so concrete can expand and contract.

PS.5.2 · Motion and Stability: Forces and Interactions · PS.5.2.1

Forces can make objects start, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.

NC objective PS.5.2.1: Carry out investigations to explain how factors such as gravity, friction, and change in mass affect the motion of objects.

I can run a fair test to show how friction and mass change how far an object moves.

Big idea

Forces can make objects start, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction. Gravity pulls everything toward the center of the Earth. Friction works against motion when surfaces rub. A heavier object needs a bigger force to change its motion.

Key ideas
  • Gravity is a non-contact force that pulls objects toward Earth's center.
  • Friction is a contact force. It opposes motion and slows things down.
  • Rougher surfaces make more friction; smoother surfaces make less.
  • More mass means the same push causes less change in motion.
  • In a fair test, change only one variable at a time and run more than one trial.

PS.5.2 · Motion and Stability: Forces and Interactions · PS.5.2.2

You can describe motion with numbers: how far something travels in a certain amount of time.

NC objective PS.5.2.2: Use mathematics and computational thinking to infer the motion of an object (including position, direction, and speed).

I can make and read a distance–time graph to tell when something is moving, when it is stopped, and which object is faster.

Big idea

You can describe motion with numbers: how far something travels in a certain amount of time. Put time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis. A rising line means moving. A flat line means stopped. A steeper line means faster.

Key ideas
  • Motion can be measured by the distance traveled in an amount of time.
  • On a distance–time graph, time goes on the horizontal (x) axis and distance on the vertical (y) axis.
  • Flat line = stopped. Steeper line = faster.
  • Match data tables with the graphs they make.
  • Focus on understanding motion, not calculating speed formulas.

LS.5.1 · From Molecules to Organisms · LS.5.1.1

Your body is built in levels.

NC objective LS.5.1.1: Use models to recognize the organizational structure of humans as a multicellular organism (cell, tissue, organ, system, organism).

I can put the levels of organization in order: cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, organism.

Big idea

Your body is built in levels. Many cells make tissues. Tissues make organs, like the heart. Organs work together in organ systems, like the circulatory system. All the systems together make you, the organism.

Key ideas
  • Multicellular organisms are made of many different parts.
  • cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism
  • A diagram or model can show this hierarchy.
  • You don't need to name cell parts or tissue types at this level.

LS.5.1 · From Molecules to Organisms · LS.5.1.2

Six systems keep you alive.

NC objective LS.5.1.2: Use models to compare the major systems of the human body (digestive, respiratory, circulatory, muscular, skeletal, nervous) as it relates to their functions necessary for life.

I can name the main parts and jobs of six body systems and compare how they work together.

Big idea

Six systems keep you alive. Circulatory: heart, blood, vessels move oxygen and nutrients. Respiratory: nose, trachea, lungs swap oxygen and carbon dioxide. Digestive: mouth to intestines break down food. Skeletal: bones support and protect. Muscular: muscles move you. Nervous: brain, spinal cord, nerves send signals.

Key ideas
  • Circulatory: heart, blood, vessels. Moves blood, oxygen and nutrients; removes waste.
  • Respiratory: nose, trachea, lungs. Breathes in oxygen, breathes out carbon dioxide.
  • Muscular: muscles. Movement, posture, balance.
  • Skeletal: bones. Structure, support, protection.
  • Digestive: mouth, esophagus, stomach, intestines. Breaks food into nutrients.
  • Nervous: brain, spinal cord, nerves. Sends signals between brain and body.
  • Voluntary muscles you control (leg muscles); involuntary ones work on their own (heart, stomach).

ESS.5.1 · Earth's Systems · ESS.5.1.4

The sun powers the water cycle.

NC objective ESS.5.1.4: Use models to explain how the sun's energy drives the processes of the water cycle (including evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation).

I can use a particle model to explain how the sun's energy drives evaporation, transpiration, condensation and precipitation.

Big idea

The sun powers the water cycle. When sunlight heats water, its particles vibrate faster and spread apart, so the water evaporates into invisible water vapor. Plants release water vapor too (transpiration). When vapor cools, the particles slow down and move closer together, forming droplets (condensation) in clouds and dew. Droplets join and fall as precipitation.

Key ideas
  • Heat added: particles vibrate faster and move apart. Solid → liquid → gas.
  • Heat removed: particles slow down and move closer. Gas → liquid → solid.
  • Evaporated water is in the air as invisible water vapor.
  • Clouds, dew, fog and the drops on a cold glass are condensation.
  • More sun energy → more evaporation. More transpiration in summer and in daytime.

Science words

Click a word to see what it means.

evaporation

Liquid water turning into water vapor.

transpiration

Plants releasing water vapor through their leaves.

condensation

Water vapor cooling into liquid droplets.

precipitation

Water falling from clouds.

water vapor

Water as an invisible gas.

particles

Tiny bits of matter too small to see.

dew

Droplets that condense on cool surfaces.

sun's energy

Heat and light from the sun that power the water cycle.

Quick check

Answer each question in your head or notebook, then click Check answer.

1. Why do wet clothes dry faster on a hot summer day?

  1. The water particles get more energy and evaporate faster
  2. The clothes get lighter
  3. The wind is colder
Check answer

A. The water particles get more energy and evaporate faster: More energy means more evaporation.

2. Drops form on a cold glass of lemonade on a hot day. This is…

  1. Evaporation
  2. Condensation
  3. Transpiration
Check answer

B. Condensation: Water vapor in the air cools on the glass and becomes liquid.

3. When heat is removed from water vapor, its particles…

  1. Move faster and spread apart
  2. Slow down and move closer together
Check answer

B. Slow down and move closer together: Cooling slows particles, and gas becomes liquid.
